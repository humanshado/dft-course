The Exponential Wall

Why the many-electron wavefunction is hopeless, and why Quantum ESPRESSO never stores one.

Your win for this lesson Estimate how much memory a many-electron wavefunction would need, and explain in one sentence why DFT works with the electron density instead.

One electron: a cloud you can store

A single electron is described by a wavefunction ψ(r), a function of one position r = (x, y, z). To put it on a computer, sample it on a grid with M points along each axis. That takes M × M × M = M3 numbers. With M = 10, that's a thousand numbers. Easy.

Two electrons: a grid of grids

Two electrons don't get two separate wavefunctions. They share one wavefunction ψ(r1, r2), because they repel each other, so the amplitude for electron 2 depends on where electron 1 is. That means that for every grid point of electron 1, you need a whole grid for electron 2:

Each electron you add nests the whole picture inside every cell again. For N electrons, the wavefunction depends on 3N coordinates:

numbers to store ψ = M3N

I'm ignoring spin here. Including it multiplies the count by at most 2N, which makes the wall even steeper but doesn't change the story.

Walter Kohn called this the exponential wall (Nobel lecture, §II.B). N sits in the exponent, so every extra electron multiplies the cost by another factor of M3.

What survives: the density

Now ask a simpler question: on average, how many electrons are near the point r? That's the electron density n(r). You get it by taking |ψ|² and integrating out every electron except one:

n(r) = N ∫ |ψ(r, r2, …, rN)|² dr2 … drN

The factor N appears because electrons are identical, so any of them could be the one at r. Integrate n(r) over all space and you get N, the total number of electrons.

However many electrons there are, n(r) is a function of three coordinates. On the same grid it always costs M3 numbers. That's the blue bar in the chart above, and it doesn't move when you change N.

The bet DFT makes

Going from ψ to n looks like it throws away a huge amount of information. The surprising result at the heart of DFT, the Hohenberg–Kohn theorem (Lesson 2), is that for the ground state nothing essential is lost: the density alone determines the energy and every other ground-state property.

For your thesis work, this is why Quantum ESPRESSO can run on a laptop at all. A pw.x run stores the density (and a handful of 3D one-electron functions) on a 3D grid. It never stores a 3N-dimensional wavefunction. When you do your first calculation, the output will report the size of that 3D grid ("FFT dimensions").
